AI Speeds Up AdaptiveWork Delivery,
but Only If It Knows AdaptiveWork

By John Mallory, PMP, MITM

Ask a general-purpose AI to help with an AdaptiveWork configuration and you'll get a fluent, confident, well-organized answer. Some of it will be right. Some of it won't work in the platform, and you often can't tell which is which until you're deep into the build.

That gap is what this post is really about. AI can make AdaptiveWork delivery faster and better, and I'll walk through six places where it does. But the value doesn't come from the AI alone. It comes from an AI that has been taught how AdaptiveWork actually behaves, working alongside a consultant who knows when to doubt it. That combination isn't something you can reproduce by typing a good prompt.

The Problem With a Generic AI

AdaptiveWork is a specialized enterprise platform with its proprietary configuration tool, unique functions, and design idiosyncrasies you only discover by running into them. A general-purpose AI has far less to draw on for a platform like this than for a mainstream one, so it fills the gaps with what's true of other tools. The result is answers that sound authoritative but describe options that don't exist, approaches that can't work as described, or logic that looks right and fails when it runs.

The tone never changes, either. A wrong answer arrives with the same confidence as a right one. Someone who knows AdaptiveWork well will catch many of the errors, but then you've spent the time you were trying to save. Someone who doesn't know it well won't catch them at all.

What I Did About It

Over many months, I've worked through real AdaptiveWork configuration challenges with AI, and I've treated every wrong answer as something to fix at the source. I corrected the errors, worked out why they were wrong, and built up repositories of configurations that have been checked against how the platform actually behaves. The result is an AI agent trained on AdaptiveWork's unique behaviors and idiosyncrasies, not just its feature list.

That's the part you can't get by opening a chat window. The agent carries months of corrections and verified work with it, and it's used by someone who can tell when an answer needs a second look.

Six Places a Trained Agent Earns Its Keep

1. Change impact analysis for a new request

Say a stakeholder asks for a new approval step when a project's status changes. Before anyone builds anything, a few questions need answers. Which fields does that status drive? Which business rules fire when it changes? Which custom actions, panels, and views reference those fields? What reports depend on them?

The traditional approach is for an admin to search each potentially-impacted area and hope nothing gets missed. With an agent that understands how AdaptiveWork behaves, you can ask those questions directly and get a first-pass map of the components involved, the rules that might cascade, and the questions worth taking back to the stakeholder. The training matters here. A generic AI will happily describe how a change ripples through a platform, but for AdaptiveWork it can only guess. You still verify the result, but you're checking a map instead of drawing one from scratch. It's also the best defense against the rule-triggers-rule chain reactions I wrote about in The Configuration Trap.

2. Documenting what exists, and keeping it current

A data dictionary is one of the most valuable deliverables on any implementation and one of the most neglected. A trained agent can draft it directly from the configuration: field names and types, picklist values, where each field is used, and a plain-language description of what each rule does, in the platform's own terms. A consultant then reviews it and adds the part only people know, which is the business reason behind each piece. Because the agent knows where AdaptiveWork's behavior is easy to misread, it can also point to the places that deserve a human look instead of papering over them.

The result is documentation produced as a byproduct of the work instead of an end-of-project chore, and it can be regenerated after each change. It's also a direct answer to the question I think every configuration should face: who will maintain this after I'm gone?

3. Comparing environments

Anyone who has managed multiple AdaptiveWork environments knows how easily they drift. Although the Migrate and Deploy tool can be utilized to create a comparison report, the results can require hours of analysis to determine which differences are trivial (e.g. custom action order), which are intentional (reference to a user who only exists in the specific environment), and which are real differences in functionality. Comparing them by hand is tedious and error-prone. An agent can compare the configuration from two environments and explain the differences in plain language: what's different, which differences are part of the pending release, and which look like drift or a missed step. Knowing the platform is what separates a useful comparison from a noisy one, because a trained agent is better at telling meaningful differences from expected ones.

That's valuable before you promote a change, and just as valuable when you're chasing a "works in sandbox, fails in production" problem.

4. Turning requirements into test plans

Given documented requirements and the configuration built to meet them, an agent can draft test cases: the expected path, edge cases, permission variations, and negative tests. That gives you a test plan traced to requirements early in the project, rather than a rushed one at the end. Platform knowledge matters most here, because the best test cases target the places AdaptiveWork configurations tend to break, and a generic tool wouldn't know to probe them. An experienced consultant then prunes the plan and adds the cases that depend on your data and your users.

5. Health checks and cleanup

Unused fields, rules that check for scenarios that no longer exist, duplicate logic, and long chains of dependent automation are hard to find by hand in a mature environment. A trained agent is good at surfacing candidates, and it recognizes the patterns that cause performance and maintenance trouble on this platform, not just general untidiness. The key word is candidates. Someone still has to confirm that a field isn't feeding a quarterly report or an integration before it goes anywhere near the delete button.

6. Adoption: training and communication

An implementation only succeeds if people use it. An agent can turn your documented configuration and release notes into role-based quick-reference guides, plain-language summaries of what changed, and draft training outlines written for each audience. Because they're built from your actual configuration and from how the platform really behaves, users aren't taught things that don't work the way the guide says. A consultant makes sure the material matches how your organization really works.

Even a Trained Agent Needs a Consultant

Training reduces mistakes. It doesn't eliminate them, and AI can still be confidently wrong. It can read a configuration, but it doesn't know why a rule exists, which stakeholder will push back, or what your organization is willing to change. The quality of its answers also depends on what it can see. If it only has part of your configuration, expect a partial picture.

My rule is simple: AI proposes, a consultant verifies, and nothing is changed or deployed without that review. Decisions about a production system stay with people who are accountable for them.

There's one more consideration. Your configuration is information about how your business runs. Before pointing any AI tool at your environment, be clear about what data it will see and where that data goes, and check it against your organization's policies.

What This Means for Your Project

You can absolutely experiment with AI on your own AdaptiveWork environment, and I'd encourage the curiosity. Just treat anything it tells you about the platform as a hypothesis until someone who knows AdaptiveWork has checked it. If you'd rather skip the trial and error, that's what I've built: a trained agent, a verified body of configuration knowledge, and the experience to know when to trust it. The payoff is a project that moves faster and has fewer surprises along the way.

If you're planning a new implementation, or you've inherited an environment nobody fully understands, I'd be glad to talk through what this approach could look like for you.
